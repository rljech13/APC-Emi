Interaction of cell-cycle regulation anaphase promoting complex (APC) and Emi2 protein in parthenogenetic lizards (Darevskia, Lacertidae) of hybrid origin. 
 
 
Ilya Rubinshteyn 
Oleg Nikolaev 
Irena Martirosyan 
Edward A. Galoyan[a]

Abstract 
Oocyte meiosis suspends at metaphase II until fertilization in sexually reproducing animals. Cytostatic factor arrest is based on inhibition of anaphase promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) by Emi2. Matthew K. Fujita and Craig Morits in their work suggested that incompatibility between heterospecific genomes in parthenogenetic lizards leads to reduced interaction between these two protein factors resulting in spontaneous oocyte development. We further suggested that heterospecific mutations in Emi2 protein are responsible for binding to the active center of APC/C and might be the reason for spontaneous embryonic development in parthenogenetic reptiles. 
To test this hypothesis we searched for the mutations in the active center of Emi2 of sexual species of rock lizards Darevskia mixta, D. raddei nairensis and D. valentini. Different hybrid combinations between these species gave rise to several parthenogens. Though we found several non-synonymous single-nucleotide substitutions in both Emi2 and APC/C of all three species, structural modeling revealed no role of these variable sites in interaction between the proteins. 
Thus, we found no evidence of incompatibility of heterospecific APC/C and Emi2 in rock lizards, although it may still be the case for other groups of parthenogenetic reptiles. 
 
 
Keywords 
Parthenogenesis, meiosis, Emi2, APC/C, rock lizards, structural modeling 
 
 
Introduction 
Most obligatory parthenogenetic/gynogenetic fishes, amphibians and reptiles arose from hybridization between heterospecific males and females [1[b], 2[c], 3[d]]. To explain this observation, researchers suggested that genetic disbalance in the hybrid progeny between two distant phylogenetic parental lineages is crucial for the initiation of the unisexual reproduction in F1 hybrids [4[e]]. Though unisexual reproduction is widespread among vertebrates, reptiles are the only taxa capable of true parthenogenetic reproduction without oocyte activation by male sperm [5[f], 6[g], 7[h]]. This, however, does not mean that they are not capable of reproduction with males. Numerous observations of triploid and tetraploid hybrids between parthenogenetic and sexual species of Darevskia [1, 8[i]]; Leiolepis [9[j]], Aspidoscelis [10[k], 11[l]] and geckoes belonging to Lepidodactylus, Hemidactylus and Heteronotia genera [12[m], 13[n]] prove that oocytes in parthenogenetic lizard fertilisation with a sperm cell can occur in these species. 
In sexually reproducing vertebrates, oocyte meiosis suspends at metaphase II until fertilization, the phenomenon referred to as cytostatic factor (CSF) arrest [14]. The molecular mechanism of CSF arrest is based on inhibition of anaphase promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) by Emi2 (FBXO43) [15[o]]. Emi2 degrades rapidly after fertilisation, and oocyte proceeds to anaphase II [14, 16]. Gynogenetic fishes and amphibians do not rely on sperm cell genetic material for reproduction, but still require fusion for resolving CSF arrest [17] (Fig. 1). 
In sexual species cell cycle is suspended to prevent parthenogenetic development, however in clonal species this mechanism is somehow broken. Experimental knockout of Emi2 in mice made them sterile and led to parthenogenetic division of some fraction of oocytes [1[p]8]. One of the possible mechanisms leading to uninitiated cleavage of oocytes was suggested by Fujita and Morits [19[q]]. According to their theoretical discourse, genetic incompatibility between interspecific genomes may reduce the activity of Emi2. This means that unblocked APC/C is not able to prevent the egg cell from developing and fertilisation is not required. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
To specify Fujita and Morits’ theory we suggested that spontaneous embryonic development occurs in parthenogenetic females due to incompatibility and weak interaction between Emi2 of one parental species and APC/C of another one. 
Here we test the hypothesis that mutations in Emi2 protein are responsible for binding with the active center of APC/C and reduced binding probability of these two protein complexes might be the reason for spontaneous embryonic development in parthenogenetic reptiles. 
 
 
Material and Methods 
 
 
Species studied 
Rock lizards of genus Darevskia Arribas 1999 comprise about 40 species including at least six parthenogenetic lineages (Arribas, 2025[r][s][t]). These small (about 50–70 mm of body length) diurnal lizards inhabit mostly rocky outcrops in the Caucasus and adjusted mountains [29[u], 30]. All known parthenogenetic species of this genus are of hybrid origin [29, 31, 32]. 
Genus Darevskia, except basally diverged D. parvula (Lanz and Cyren 1913), is subdivided into three clades, named after their representative species: rudis, caucasica and saxicola [33]. Notably, all parthenogenetic species descended from hybridization of a paternal species from rudis clade and a maternal species from caucasica clade [32]. 
Precise parentage and phylogenetic relations of unisexual rock lizards described in the recent works [30, 33, 34, 35[v]]. According to these studies, there are three maternal species from the caucasica clade: Azerbaidjan lizard D. raddei raddei (Boettgerr 1892), Nairi rock lizard D. r. nairensis (Darevsky 1967), Adjarian lizard D. mixta (Mehely 1909) and two paternal species from rudis clade: Valentin’s lizard D. valentini (Boettgerr 1892) and D. portschinskii (Kessler 1878). Different combinations of these five species gave rise to all existing parthenogens, and some pairs of species initiated the origin of several parthenogenetic species. 
Our study was focused on three species: D. valentini, D. r. nairensis and D. mixta, as their full genome assemblies and raw genome or transcriptome data were available (see below). The first two species gave rise to D. unisexualis, D. uzzelli and D. bendimahiensis parthenogemetic species, while D. valenti and D. mixta are suggested to be parental for D. armeniaca [31; Arakelyan et al., 2025[w][x], 37[y]]. 
 
 
Sequence inference 
Meiotic APC/C inhibition by Emi2 depends on a composite C-terminal interface: the D-box contacts Cdc20 and the co-receptor Anapc10, the zinc-binding region (ZBR) contacts Anapc11, Anapc2 and Anapc1, and the C-terminal RL tail docks on Anapc2 [38[z]; 39, 40, 41]. A dual-side incompatibility between parental alleles would therefore require amino-acid variations on both Emi2 and the facing APC/C surface. To test this possibility we focused on Anapc1, Anapc2, Anapc10, Anapc11, Cdc20 and Emi2 of the parental species. 
Although genome assemblies of D. valentini, D. r. nairensis and D. mixta are available in GenBank, gene annotations were absent and the amino-acid sequences were not present. In order to obtain these gene sequences, we mapped protein sequences of Podarcis muralis (Laurenti 1768) Anapc1, Anapc2, Anapc10, Anapc11, Cdc20 and Emi2 (NCBI IDs XP_077781620.1, XP_028604892.2, XP_077790194.1, XP_028571784.1, XP_028589628.2 and XP_028592231.2 respectively) to the D. valentini genome assembly GCA_034642135.1 using tBLASTn [42] online tool. All six genes were successfully detected (Query cover > 0.89, Per. Identity > 0.65, E-value < 10-38). 
We further mapped the ovary RNAseq data of D. valentini and D. r. nairensis (SRR16549247, SRR16549248 respectively) to the inferred gene sequences with STAR [43] for exon annotation. An exon was defined as a region covered by at least 50 reads. The threshold was chosen by visual inspection of coverage. Coding sequences were successfully inferred for all genes except Anapc1 for both species. D. valentini transcriptome mapped to D. valentini gene sequences with no mismatches. In contrast, D. r. nairensis transcripts contained up to 32 point mutations per gene relative to the D. valentini references. No insertions or deletions were observed between the inferred coding sequences, consistent with correct exon boundary prediction. Mutations were introduced with bcftools [44]. 
As D. r. nairensis Anapc1 could not be recovered from transcriptome data, we extracted its gene sequence from the assembly GCA_034642065.1 as above. Unlike in D. valentini, the locus was split between contigs JAWWNI010000161.1 and JAWWNI010000761.1, likely because of a GAAA short tandem repeat array between exons 28 and 29 detectable in D. valentini. Alignment of the P. muralis Anapc1 CDS XM_077925494.1 to these gene sequences covered the full CDS in D. valentini, but left a 141 bp gap between the D. r. nairensis contigs, resulting in 47 unaligned amino acids. 
No transcriptome data were available for D. mixta, hence its coding sequences were inferred with the genome-based approach from GCA_034641385.1 assembly, as described above, except Anapc11, which gave no usable tBLASTn exon structure. D. mixta Anapc11 was therefore recovered from Illumina WGS reads (SRR36838325) mapped to the D. valentini Anapc11 gene with BWA [45] (coverage 0.985, mean depth 9334.97), followed by alignment to the P. muralis CDS and extraction of aligned regions. 
DNA sequences were aligned with the MAFFT algorithm, and protein sequences were aligned with the Clustal Omega algorithm. ORF detection and sequence alignments were performed in the Benchling online tool (https://benchling.com). Amino-acid sequences were taken from the longest ORF spanning the annotated exons. 
 
 
Molecular modelling of Emi2–APC/C contacts 
Complex screens used the four reciprocal allele combinations of Emi2 with the partner set, unless a subunit was identical between species. 
We explored global docking of hybrid pairings first. The unstructured N-terminal region was removed from the Emi2 model in Maestro [46[aa]]. Global protein–protein docking of D. valentini Emi2 with the D. r. nairensis APC/C complex, and the reciprocal pairing, was performed with ClusPro [47]. ClusPro was used only as an exploratory survey; all distances, engagement calls and dual-side tests below were made with AlphaFold3 [48]. ClusPro reports whole-complex docking poses, but not residue-resolved contacts or a per-chain confidence score that can be compared across allele combinations. We therefore used AlphaFold3 for the quantitative interface analysis, where pair_ipTM and minimum heavy-atom distances can be evaluated seed by seed. 
Multimer screens were run on the AlphaFold3 Server with default settings (twelve seeds per screen, five diffusion samples per seed). In the first screen (S1), Emi2 residues 535–675 were modelled with full-length Cdc20 and Anapc10. In the second screen (S2b), the same Emi2 fragment was modelled with the Anapc2 catalytic fragment and Anapc11. In the third screen (S3), Emi2 residues 535–675 were modelled with Anapc1 residues 950–1250. A chain pair was called engaged if pair_ipTM ≥ 0.50. 
Figure panels were assembled in Python (matplotlib); molecular graphics were rendered in ChimeraX [49]. 
 
 
Results 
Several substitutions were observed between Emi2 of bisexual species 
We observed ten substitutions between Emi2 amino acid sequences of D. valentini and D. r. nairensis: Q76H, V92I, A102T, K171R, E219D, I391V, D395N, L425F, F549I and V590M (Fig. 2a). In particular, F549I belongs to D-box, . Substitutions Q76H, V92I, A102T, K171R, D395N were also observed between D. valentini and D.mixta. In addition, we found A450V and V590A mutations between these species. Notably, amino acid at position 590 differed between all three studied species. We further wondered which of these variable sites lie on the APC/C-facing surface and could therefore be relevant to a dual-side test. 
 
 
Molecular modeling indicates possible role of Emi2 positions 549 and 590 in interaction with APC/C 
To relate variable amino-acid positions to the APC/C-binding elements of Emi2, we modelled the C-terminal part of the protein as a monomer and in complex with APC/C subunits (Fig. 2). 
Modelling of D. valentini Emi2 residues 400–675 recovered the expected architecture: F-box (452–537), D-box (548–551), ZBR (600–648) and RL tail (658–675) (Fig. 2a). The F-box is a Skp1/SCF module and is not part of the APC/C interface (Chang et al., 2015). Per-region pLDDT is high only for autonomously folded segments; the D-box and linkers fall in the very-low band, as expected for motifs that order on binding (Fig. 2b). Of the ten parental substitutions, eight lie N-terminal of residue 535. Only F549I and V590M fall in the APC/C-facing window. We therefore restricted complex modelling to Emi2 535–675. 
In screen S1 (Emi2 + Cdc20 + Anapc10), Emi2–Cdc20 was engaged in 45/60 models. To check that the S1 D-box pose was canonical, we superimposed modelled Сdc20 on Cdc20 from PDB 5G04 [40] and measured how closely the modelled Emi2 D-box matched the Hsl1 D-box peptide in that structure. Emi2 D-box occupied the canonical Cdc20 site, reproducing the Hsl1–Cdc20 geometry of PDB 5G04 (backbone RMSD; Supplementary Fig. S1). 
Anapc10 did not reach its co-receptor pose in this three-chain construct and was excluded from the distance analysis. In S2b (Emi2 + Anapc2 + Anapc11), the ZBR is packed against Anapc2/Anapc11 (50/60 engaged for Emi2–Anapc11), consistent with the previously described ZBR-facing surface. In S3 (Emi2 + Anapc1 950–1250), 0/60 models were engaged; Anapc1 was excluded from the distance analysis. 
For engaged models we recorded the minimum heavy-atom distance from each Emi2 residue in 535–675 to each partner and took the median across engaged models. Interfaces without engagement (Anapc10 in S1; Anapc1 in S3) were excluded from the distance statistics. 
In engaged S1 models the measurable approach of residue 549 (F/I) is to Cdc20 (any-atom median 3.7 Å), however, it is backbone: the side chain never reached ≤ 4 Å of Cdc20 (0/45). Allele swaps showed no effect of Phe versus Ile on Emi2–Cdc20 engagement. 
In engaged S2b models residue 590 (V/M) approaches Anapc11 at 6.5–6.9 Å and Anapc2 at 13.5 Å; the side chain never reached ≤4 Å (0/50) (Fig. 2c). 
 
 
 
 
Variable Emi2 positions make contacts with conservative positions on APC/C 
A parental variable site in Emi2 can support a dual-side incompatibility only if it contacts a parental variable site on the APC/C side of the interface. We therefore surveyed substitutions in Anapc1, Anapc2, Anapc10, Anapc11 and Cdc20. Among them, Anapc10 and Anapc11 were identical in D. valentini, D. r. nairensis and D. mixta. Anapc2 differed at two positions between D. valentini and both D. r. nairensis and D. mixta. Cdc20 differed at one position between D. valentini and D. r. nairensis, and at two positions between D. valentini and D. mixta. Anapc1 carried five single-residue substitutions between D. valentini and both other species, a 12-amino-acid C-terminal change caused by a frameshift at the end of the CDS, and a 47-amino-acid unread stretch in D. r. nairensis due to the assembly gap. 
In order to place APC/C-side variable positions relative to Emi2 variable positions, we computed the minimum heavy-atom distance to the nearest in-construct Emi2 substitution (F549I or V590M) for each residue of Cdc20, Anapc1, Anapc2 and plotted soft proximity profiles. 
None of Cdc20, Anapc1 and Anapc2 variable positions contacted variable F549I or V590M Emi2 positions in the interfaces we measured. In the engaged AlphaFold3 models the dual-side intersection – an Emi2 substitution inside a tested contact that faces a variable APC/C residue was empty (Fig. 2c). From the partner side, parental APC/C substitutions lied far from the two in-construct Emi2 sites (F549I and V590M): median minimum heavy-atom distances were ~36 Å for Cdc20 V291I, ~42 Å for Anapc2 V632A and ~59 Å for Anapc2 P654L (Supplementary Fig. S2). Anapc10 and Anapc11 are identical between parents, and Anapc10 interface was not recovered in S1. Closing Anapc10 and Anapc1 geometry will require larger constructs. 
 
 
Discussion 
Here we describe the spatial configuration of APC/C and Emi2 complex for lizards. Though these complexes play a great role in regulation of meiosis in animals and are well-described for some mammals [14, 16], before our study there was no such a description for reptiles. Whether parthenogenetic oocyte development relies on incompatibility between Emi2 and APC/C of parental species, mutations must be observed in both Emi2 and its counterparts on APC/C. Moreover, either conformation of both Emi2 and APC/C must differ significantly between species, or variable amino-acid positions on both sides of the interface must interact. Otherwise, Emi2 and APC/C would be incompatible within parental species as well. 
In order to test our hypothesis, we first annotated and predicted structures of Anapc1, Anapc2, Anapc10, Anapc11, Emi2 and Cdc20 proteins of bisexual species D. valentini, D. r. nairensis and D. mixta. Unfortunately, no data are currently available to infer sequences of these proteins from unisexual D. unisexualis and D. armeniaca. In addition, we currently do not know if parental proteins are expressed equally in parthenogenetic species or not. High quality transcriptomic data are required to solve these issues. 
Although we have found two possibly functional substitutions in the Emi2, both of them cannot influence binding between the studied proteins, as conformation of Emi2 is invariant among species and distances between variable positions on Emi2 and APC/C in the interface are too large for interaction. Thus, our results prove that spontaneous cleavage of duplicated oocytes in rock lizards [50] is not affected by incompatibility of heterospecific APC/C and Emi2 protein complexes. This, however, doesn’t mean that this mechanism cannot take place in other groups of unisexual species. We suggest that SNPs found in studied species resemble their phylogenetic relations but do not affect protein functions. It is more likely that the nature of spontaneous oocyte development in parthenogenetic lizards is explained by more distal mechanisms, which may include regulators of Emi2 activity and initial stages of second meiotic cell division [51[ab]].

Figure legends 
 
 
Fig. 1. Schematic representation of CSF arrest molecular mechanism. Prior to fertilization, APC/C complex is bound to Emi2 protein, which inhibits its association with Ube2S ubiquitin ligase [20]. Emi2 activity is regulated via phosphorylation. It can be inactivated and directed to proteasomal degradation by CycB/Cdk1 complex, but the phosphorylation can be reverted by PP2A phosphorylase, a terminal effector of Mos/MAPK pathway [21, 22, 23]. Therefore, there is a dynamic equilibrium between active and inactive states of Emi2 during meiosis II metaphase. 
At fertilization, phospholipase PLCζ is injected to an oocyte from an acrosome, and cleaves phosphatidylinositol 1,3-bisphosphate (IP2) at the inner layer of plasma membrane producing soluble inositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3). IP3 binds to IP3R Ca2+ channels at the endoplasmic reticulum, leading to a burst of Ca2+ in cytoplasm. This in turn leads to activation of CaMKII kinase, which promotes Emi2 proteasomal degradation via phosphorylation [24]. 
Next, Ube2S binds APC/C complex resulting in ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of CycB and Securin, inhibitors of Separase [25, 26, 27]. Finally, Separase activation promotes anaphase II onset via Cohesin cleavage [25].
